# YouTube Strategy — Pre-Production

**This runs before rule 01 (scene structure). Before any narration. Before any bullets.**

Average creators pick a topic and start writing. Top creators spend a large share of their
time here — on title, thesis, thumbnail, and architecture — before a single word of script.
Modern YouTube is **packaging-first**: the title and thumbnail are decided early, and the
video is built to deliver on them. The script serves the strategy, not the other way around.

All examples below show the PATTERN, not content to copy. Apply each pattern to YOUR
topic's real, researched facts (rule 00).

## Step 0.5 — Competitor gap analysis (before writing the title)

Search your topic on YouTube before writing a single word. This is the step that
guarantees your angle is non-redundant.

```
1. Search your topic on YouTube (exact phrase + variations)
2. Watch the first 60 seconds of the top 3 results
3. Write down:
   (a) What angle did each video take?
   (b) What did they skip or gloss over?
   (c) What are the top complaints in their comment sections?
```

Your angle must be what they skipped, or what the comments complained was missing.

If the top results already cover your exact angle, you have two options: go deeper
(primary sources, newer data, more specific numbers) or reframe entirely (different
thesis, audience, or failure mode).

```
Competitors watched:
  [title 1] — angle: [X], skipped: [Y]
  [title 2] — angle: [X], skipped: [Y]
  [title 3] — angle: [X], skipped: [Y]
My angle: [what none of them covered]
```

Only proceed when you have a documented gap.

## Step 1 — Write the title BEFORE the script

The title is not a label for the finished video. It is a **creative brief** for the
script. Write it first; everything in the script earns its place by serving the title's
promise.

### Title patterns

The highest-performing explainer titles follow one of these:

| Pattern | What it does | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Contradiction** | two true things that seem to clash | seems wrong → must watch to resolve |
| **Impossible number** | a specific surprising figure + a promise to explain | specificity + curiosity |
| **The vs with a twist** | a familiar comparison, reframed | overturns the obvious matchup |
| **The thing nobody explains** | "the real reason X…" | promises a unique angle |
| **The hidden cost / stakes** | a surprising consequence | stakes + specificity |
| **The first-person result** | "I did X — here's what happened" | personal + specific + unexpected |

Apply the pattern to your topic's hook fact from research (rule 00). The title comes from
what you found — not from picking a pattern and filling in generic words.

### Title quality checklist
- [ ] Contains a number OR a contradiction OR "why/how/actually/real"
- [ ] Specific enough that a viewer knows EXACTLY what the video covers
- [ ] Creates a question the viewer wants answered
- [ ] Avoids "everything you need to know," "ultimate guide," "beginner's guide"
- [ ] Understandable in ~3 seconds — no jargon that needs domain knowledge
- [ ] Under ~60 characters (YouTube truncates in search)

### Test it
Ask: *"With a strong thumbnail, would I click this over the other results?"* If uncertain,
rewrite it. A weak title means weak views regardless of content quality.

## Step 1b — Click confirmation

The title makes a promise. Scene 1 must CONFIRM that promise within the first few seconds.
The viewer's first question is *"Did I click the right video?"* — answer YES immediately.
Viewers who suspect clickbait leave in under 5 seconds.

- If the title states a contradiction → Scene 1 states it cold, no preamble.
- If the title leads with a number → Scene 1 opens on that number, no introduction.
- If the title promises a result → Scene 1 shows the result, doesn't introduce you.

**Check:** read only Scene 1, sentence 1. Does it confirm the title's promise? If not,
rewrite sentence 1.

## Step 2 — Identify the thumbnail moment BEFORE writing

The thumbnail is the #1 driver of click-through rate, alongside the title. It's designed
before the script, and the script then creates that moment.

### High-CTR thumbnail options (animation pipeline)
- **The conflict visual** — two entities side by side, strong contrast, one clearly ahead.
- **The impossible number** — a single large figure that seems wrong, on a dark background.
- **The before/after** — left = bad/chaos, right = good/clean, a hard divider between.

(A face + reaction thumbnail is highest-CTR generally, but doesn't apply to an
animation-only pipeline — use one of the three above.)

### Composition rules
- **Size test:** it must read at ~120×68px (mobile). If the key element vanishes that
  small, redesign. This is the most common thumbnail failure.
- **Text rule:** max ~3 words. More is unreadable small — let the visual carry it.
- **One emotion:** pick one (danger / success / competition / surprise). A thumbnail
  saying two things says nothing.
- **The squint test:** blur it in your mind. If the key element (number, contrast) is
  gone, redesign.

### Decide before writing
```
Thumbnail moment: Scene [N], Bullet [M]
Visual: [the thumbnail frame in one sentence]
Element shown: [the specific number / contrast visible]
Emotion: [one word]
Passes squint test: yes / no
```
Then make sure the script ACTUALLY creates that frame — the thumbnail drives a script
requirement.

## Step 3 — Write the thesis (not the topic)

**Topic:** what the video is about. **Thesis:** what the video ARGUES.
Top creators have a thesis; average creators have topics.

A strong thesis:
- Takes a side (not "both have pros and cons")
- Would make some informed viewers disagree (mild controversy = comments)
- Fits in one sentence
- Is NOT obvious from a quick search of the topic
- Is something you actually believe from research, not a clickbait take

### Falsifiability test
> Could a reasonable, informed person watch this and disagree with the thesis?
> If no, it's not a thesis — it's a summary. A thesis needs a counterargument to exist.

A claim like "X is important" fails (no one disagrees). A claim that reframes or takes a
specific position passes (someone could present counter-data).

```
THESIS: [one sentence — what this video argues]
```
Every scene must serve this thesis. If a scene doesn't advance the argument — cut it.

## Step 4 — Design the open loops

An **open loop** is a question raised and intentionally left unanswered. Viewers holding
an unanswered question cannot stop watching. Plant several; the final scene resolves the
most important one.

- **Hook loop (Scene 1):** the central question/contradiction the whole video answers.
- **Planted loop (early body):** a secondary question raised mid-video, answered 2–3 scenes later.
- **Mid-video loop (~40%):** the most surprising fact dropped here, its consequence held for later.
- **Pre-CTA loop (second-to-last scene):** a question the final scene resolves.

```
Loop 1 (Hook):       Scene 1     → resolved Scene [N]
Loop 2 (Planted):    Scene [X]   → resolved Scene [X+2]
Loop 3 (Mid-video):  Scene [~40%] → resolved Scene [~60%]
Loop 4 (Pre-CTA):    Scene [N-1] → resolved Scene [N]
```
Then check that the scenes actually plant and resolve each loop. Never leave all loops
closed before the end (rule 01).

---

## Step 4b — Retention cliff checklist

Viewers drop off at predictable points. Each needs a structural fix.

| Cliff | When | Why they leave | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Click test** | 0–30s | "Did I click the right video?" | Scene 1 confirms the title promise in sentence 1 |
| **"I know this" exit** | ~30% | feels ahead of the script | drop a surprise/reframe at 30% |
| **"I got what I came for" exit** | ~60% | got the main point | resolve a loop AND open a new one |
| **Pre-CTA skip** | ~90% | skipping the ending | put the sharpest insight in the second-to-last scene |

```
- [ ] Scene 1 confirms the title promise in sentence 1
- [ ] A reframe/surprise exists at the ~30% scene
- [ ] A loop resolves + a new one opens at ~60%
- [ ] The second-to-last scene holds the sharpest single insight
```

## Step 5 — Identify the one shareable insight

Every video that spreads has ONE insight viewers want to tell someone else. One framework,
one fact, or one reframe — not three. One. (See rule 06 Test 12 / rule 05a.)

```
SHAREABLE INSIGHT: [the one thing viewers will repeat to a colleague tomorrow]
```
Then check the script delivers it with emphasis — a `<pause>` after it in narration and a
REPLACE visual that makes it impossible to miss.

## Step 6 — Pre-script strategy summary

Before proceeding to rule 01 (scene structure), have these written:

```
TITLE:              [final title under ~60 chars]
THESIS:             [one sentence — what the video argues]
ANGLE:              [the documented competitor gap from Step 0.5]
THUMBNAIL:          Scene N, Bullet M — [the frame]
SHAREABLE INSIGHT:  [the one thing viewers will repeat]
OPEN LOOPS:
  Hook:    Scene 1    → resolved Scene [N]
  Planted: Scene [X]  → resolved Scene [X+2]
  Mid:     Scene [~40%] → resolved Scene [~60%]
  Pre-CTA: Scene [N-1] → resolved Scene [N]
```

If any of these is missing or weak — do not start writing scenes. A weak strategy
produces a weak video regardless of how well the script is written.
