# Step 5a — Retention Engineering — Keep Them Watching

Runs after the technical validator (rule 05) and before the human script review (rule 05b):
once the script is technically valid, engineer its retention, then judge it as a viewer.

The hook gets the click. Retention keeps the watch time. A video with a great hook and a
weak middle still fails. This covers the techniques that keep viewers past the points
where they normally leave.

## The retention curve — where viewers leave

YouTube retention graphs show predictable drop-off points:

```
100% |####
     |    ####                          <- biggest drop: first 30 seconds
 75% |        ######
     |              ########            <- second drop: mid-video sag (40-60%)
 50% |                      ##########
     |                                ####  <- third drop: pre-ending (85-90%)
 25% |                                    ####
     |________________________________________
      0%   25%    50%    75%   100%
```

Each drop has a specific cause and a specific fix.

---

## Drop 1 — The first 30 seconds (biggest loss)

The first 30 seconds is the most important part of the video — most of the audience that
will leave, leaves here.

**Cause:** the hook promised something the first 30 seconds didn't deliver.

**Fixes:**
- Confirm the title's promise in sentence 1 (rule 07 Step 1b)
- No throat-clearing: cut "in this video," "let me explain," "but first"
- Front-load the most surprising fact — don't save it for the middle
- Make a forward promise: tell the viewer what's coming so they stay for it

**Test:** watch the first 30 seconds. Is there a single moment where attention could
drop? If yes, tighten it.

---

## The 30-second hook structure

The first 30 seconds is the highest-leverage writing in the script. A reliable structure:

```
0–3s:   State the surprising/impossible fact cold — no intro, no greeting
3–10s:  Name the tension — why it seems wrong or impossible
10–20s: One real piece of evidence that proves it (number, quote, event from rule 00)
20–30s: Make the promise — what the viewer will know by the end, specific
```

Four beats: fact, tension, proof, promise. Zero throat-clearing.

**Forbidden in the first 30 seconds:** channel intro/greeting, "in this video I'll…,"
explaining the topic without tension, thanking viewers, asking for a subscribe before
earning it, background the viewer doesn't need yet.

(Fill each beat with your topic's real researched facts — never a templated example.)

## Drop 2 — The mid-video sag (40–60%)

**Cause:** the novelty wore off. The viewer got the gist and doesn't see a reason to continue.

**Fixes:**
- Pattern interrupt: change the visual style, pace, or format at the ~40% mark
- Open a new loop right as you close the old one (never let all loops close at once)
- Escalate: each scene should feel like it's building to something bigger
- Drop the second-biggest surprise around the midpoint

**Test:** at the midpoint, is there a new question, a new visual, or a new stake? If the
middle is just "more information," it will sag.

---

## Drop 3 — The pre-ending skip (85–90%)

**Cause:** the viewer senses the video is wrapping up and leaves before the payoff/CTA.

**Fixes:**
- Put the best insight in the second-to-last scene, not the last
- Tease the ending earlier: signal there's one more thing worth staying for
- Make the ending pay off the opening loop — the resolution they've waited for
- Keep the CTA short — a long outro guarantees the skip

**Test:** does the last 15% deliver a payoff, or just wrap up? If it only wraps up, add a
final insight.

---

## Pattern interrupts — the core retention tool

A pattern interrupt is any change that re-captures attention. The brain notices change;
after ~10–15 seconds of the same visual style, attention drifts. Interrupt it.

Types:
- **Visual** — change the layout, color scheme, or animation style
- **Pace** — speed up or slow down the narration rhythm
- **Format** — switch from explanation to example, or data to story
- **Audio** — a beat of silence, or a shift in music
- **Scale** — zoom way in or way out

Use a pattern interrupt every ~10–20 seconds. This is why the one-pattern-per-scene rule
(rule 01) works — each scene IS a pattern interrupt.

## Re-hooks — re-promising value mid-video

A re-hook reminds the viewer why they're still watching by re-promising value. Place one
at the start of each major section — especially right after the mid-video sag point.

A re-hook does one of these:
- Signals the best part is still ahead ("here's where it gets strange")
- Reframes what came before as setup for something bigger
- Names a common mistake the viewer will want to avoid

Keep it short and specific — a re-hook is a promise, not a recap.

## Payoff architecture — every scene raises or answers

Every body scene must do at least one of:
1. **Build tension** — raise a question, introduce a problem, establish stakes
2. **Deliver payoff** — answer a question a prior scene raised
3. **Both** — answer one question while raising a new one (ideal for body scenes)

No scene should be pure information with no question raised or answered — that's a
lecture, and viewers drop off during lectures.

**Scene audit** — for each body scene ask: what question does it RAISE? what prior
question does it ANSWER? does the viewer leave it more curious, or satisfied? The ideal
body scene answers one old question and raises one new one — constant forward momentum.

---

## The withhold principle (Zeigarnik) — don't front-load the payoff

People remember unfinished things more than finished ones — an open question pulls the
viewer forward. The corollary: **revealing your best insight early kills the reason to
keep watching.** If an early scene gives the answer/framework, the viewer has what they
came for and leaves.

Reveal early vs withhold:

| Reveal early (builds investment) | Withhold until earned |
|---|---|
| The problem / the stakes | The solution / the framework |
| The contradiction | Its resolution |
| The question | The answer |
| Evidence the problem is real | The root cause |
| The surprising fact | Why it's true |

End sections on something unresolved ("there's a number that explains this — I'll get to
it"). **Test:** cover the last scene and read the first three — if the viewer already has
what they came for, you front-loaded; move the payoff later (past the ~60% mark).

## The strong-ending formula

The last 60 seconds are the second-highest-leverage writing after the hook — they decide
subscribes and whether the next video gets watched. Three parts:

1. **Resolve the hook loop** — answer the exact question Scene 1 raised. The ANSWER, not a summary.
2. **Give the decision rule** — the one actionable takeaway, with conditions ("if X, do A;
   if Y, do B"), never a flat "it depends."
3. **An earned CTA** — after delivering real value, a short subscribe ask that references
   something specific coming next, not generic "subscribe for more."

**Forbidden in the ending:** summarizing what was just covered, "that's all for today,"
a generic subscribe ask, ending on information with no verdict, or trailing off. The last
sentence must land with weight.

---

## Guidelines

### Always
- Confirm the title promise in the first sentence
- Use a pattern interrupt every ~10–20 seconds
- Keep at least one open loop unresolved at all times
- Escalate stakes toward the end; put the best insight near the end, not the start
- Place a re-hook right after the mid-video sag

### Never
- Save the best fact for last (viewers won't reach it)
- Let two adjacent scenes use the same visual pattern
- Let all open loops close before the final scene
- End on a long outro or unearned CTA
