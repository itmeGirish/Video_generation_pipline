# Step 3 — Scene Structure Design

Before writing narration, map the whole video as a scene list.
A bad scene structure produces a video that feels like a lecture.
A good scene structure produces a video that feels like a story.

This is how experienced YouTube writers think: the scene list is a **retention plan**,
not a table of contents. Every scene exists to keep the viewer watching to the next one.

## How experienced YouTube writers structure scenes — the core principles

Five principles, drawn from how high-retention creators actually build scripts:

1. **Every scene re-hooks.** A scene does not just deliver information — it raises a
   question OR answers a prior one. If a scene does neither, cut it. No "valleys"
   where the viewer's attention can drop.

2. **Loops chain, they don't sit.** Don't open one curiosity gap at the start and
   resolve it only at the end. Open and close a chain of loops: resolve one while
   opening the next, so there is always an unanswered question pulling the viewer
   forward.

3. **Tension then release.** Build a problem, stake, or question — then deliver the
   answer. The rhythm of tension→release is what creates the emotional pull. A flat
   string of facts has no rhythm.

4. **Front-load the strongest material.** The most surprising fact, the best visual,
   the sharpest line go early. Don't save everything for the end — viewers who leave
   in the first minute never reach it.

5. **Stakes escalate.** Each body scene should feel slightly higher-stakes or more
   surprising than the last, building toward the turn. Avoid a structure where scene
   3 is more interesting than scene 5.

---

## The writing order — payoffs first, hook last

Experienced writers do NOT write top-to-bottom. The proven order:

1. **Payoffs** — decide what each scene ultimately delivers (the answer, the reveal).
2. **Setups** — work backward: what does the viewer need before each payoff lands?
3. **Loops & tension** — place the questions that make each payoff feel earned.
4. **Hook last** — write Scene 1 only after the body exists. Once you know exactly
   what the video delivers, the hook almost writes itself.

Designing the scene list, build it payoff-first too: write each scene's "Answers/Delivers"
column before its title. A scene with no clear payoff is a scene with no reason to exist.

## Scene count by video length

| Target length | Scene count | Avg scene length |
|---|---|---|
| 3–5 min | 4–5 scenes | ~45–60s each |
| 8–12 min | 7–9 scenes | ~70–90s each |
| 15–20 min | 10–12 scenes | ~90–100s each |

Scenes shorter than 30s feel rushed. Scenes longer than 120s lose attention.
If a topic needs more than 120s, split it into two scenes with different angles.

**For first 10 videos on a new channel:** target the lower end of the scene count.
Fewer scenes means more time per idea, easier production, and more time to get each
animation right. 7 scenes is better than 9 when you're still building the pipeline.

## Scene arc — required structure

Every video follows this arc regardless of topic:

```
Scene 1:    COLD OPEN / HOOK        — The surprising fact. The question. The tension.
Scene 2:    SETUP / CONTEXT         — Why this matters. The stakes. The promise of payoff.
Scene 3–N:  BODY                    — One idea per scene. Escalating stakes. Loop chain.
Scene N-1:  THE TURN                — The unexpected angle. The thing no other video covers.
Scene N:    PAYOFF + CTA            — Answer the hook. Decision rule. Tell the viewer what to do.
```

The hook in Scene 1 must be answered in Scene N. If the hook asks a question,
Scene N answers it. If the hook shows a contradiction, Scene N explains it.

**Scene 2 is not a history lesson.** Cut background the viewer already knows. Setup
exists to raise the stakes and promise the payoff — not to recite context.

## Open-loop chain — the retention spine

Map your loops before writing narration. The video should carry several at once:

- **The main loop** — opened in Scene 1, closed in Scene N. The spine of the video.
- **A planted loop** — a question raised early, paid off mid-video.
- **A mid-video loop** — opened around the midpoint to survive the attention dip.
- **A pre-CTA loop** — a final tease so the ending feels earned, not tacked on.

Rule of thumb: **never let all loops be closed at once before the end.** At every
moment there should be at least one unanswered question the viewer is staying for.

For each scene in the list, note: which loop does it OPEN, which loop does it CLOSE?
A scene that does neither is a candidate to cut.

## Scene transitions — carry the thread across the cut

The cut between two scenes is exactly where viewer attention leaks. A transition is the
**connective tissue** that carries a thread across that cut so the next scene feels
inevitable ("because of that…"), not arbitrary ("and now, another thing…"). Transitions
are written into the narration at each scene boundary AND reinforced by the visual
handoff — they are a retention tool, not decoration.

**Write a bridge at every scene boundary.** The last line of a scene should set up the
next; the first line of the next should pay that setup off. Never end a scene on a full
stop and start the next from cold.

Transition types (pick the one that fits the boundary):

- **Question handoff** — end a scene by raising the question the next answers
  ("…but that raised a bigger problem."). The strongest default.
- **Cliffhanger / tease** — withhold the payoff and promise it next ("…and the reason
  is stranger than it sounds.").
- **Callback** — open the next scene by referring back to something just shown, so it
  feels continuous, not reset.
- **Cause-and-effect** — "because of that…" — the next scene is the consequence of this
  one (the story spine, rule 01b).
- **Contrast / turn** — pivot on "but," "except," "here's what nobody saw coming" to
  flip into the turn scene.
- **Match / motion handoff** — the visual carries over: an element from this scene's last
  beat persists or morphs into the next scene's first beat (a shape, a number, a color).

**Visual handoff (pairs with the bullets, rule 03):** decide whether each boundary is a
hard REPLACE (clean break to a new world — use at a topic change / the turn) or a
carry-over where an element persists across the cut (use to signal "same thread,
continued"). A smooth crossfade or a match-cut on a shared element reads as continuity;
a hard cut reads as a chapter break. Choose deliberately — both are tools.

**The boundary test:** read only the last line of each scene and the first line of the
next, back to back. Does it flow as one thought? If it reads as two unrelated statements,
the transition is missing — add a bridge. (This is what the continuity check in rule 05b
Q9 verifies on the finished script.)

In the scene list, note the transition INTO each scene so the bridge is planned, not
improvised:
```
Scene 3  "First Idea"   ← transition in: question handoff from S2's stat
Scene 4  "The Turn"     ← transition in: contrast ("but the data says the opposite")
```

## Retention checkpoints — where viewers leave

Design the structure around the three moments where viewers actually drop:

- **The first 15 seconds** — most viewers decide here whether to keep watching. Scene 1
  must land the hook fast: no slow intro, no channel preamble, no "in this video."
- **The ~2-minute mark** — the steepest mid-video drop. Place a strong re-hook,
  reveal, or pattern change near here. Don't let scene 2–3 sag into setup.
- **Every 30–90 seconds** — an engagement beat: a new visual pattern, a reveal, a
  question, or a stake change. The animation-pattern-per-scene rule below enforces
  this rhythm visually.

If the retention curve drops at one of these, the fix is structural — fix it in the
scene list, not in the narration polish.

## One idea per scene — strict rule

Every scene header title must complete this sentence:
> "This scene explains ___________."

If the blank requires "and" — split the scene.

**Bad (two ideas):**
```
## SCENE 4 — "Benchmarks and What People Say" (2:18 – 4:10)
```

**Good (one idea each):**
```
## SCENE 4 — "The Benchmark Track" (2:18 – 4:10)
## SCENE 5 — "What People Actually Say" (4:10 – 5:30)
```

---

## Scene title naming rules

Scene titles must be memorable and specific:
- Name the visual metaphor if one exists: `"The Race Track"`, `"The Lie Detector"`, `"The Transfer"`
- Name the tension: `"Why the Strongest Option Fails the Most"`
- Name the comparison or the stakes, not the topic
- Avoid generic labels: `"Section 3"`, `"Main Content"`, `"The Data"` ← all bad

Each scene's narration should contain 2–4 distinctive phrases that animation bullets
use as `audio_anchor` triggers. Plan these when designing the scene — they are the
moments where a visual fires. A scene with no plannable anchor phrase is too vague.

---

## Time windows

Scene time windows `(M:SS – M:SS)` in the header are approximate.
They tell the visual designer how long each scene runs.
Actual timing comes from TTS duration — the parser does not enforce these times.

Guidelines:
- Estimate based on word count: ~2.5 words per second at `rate: '+15%'`
- Leave 5–10s slack per scene — TTS is never exactly the estimate
- Scene 1 (cold open): usually 20–40s regardless of total video length

---

## Scene list output format

Write the scene list as a numbered table before writing any narration. Include the
loop each scene opens/closes so the retention plan is explicit:

```
Scene list for: <video title>
Total target: <length>

1.  "Cold Open"        ~30s   Hook: [surprising fact]        Opens: main loop
2.  "Setup"            ~60s   Stakes: [why it matters]       Opens: planted loop
3.  "First Idea"       ~70s   Delivers: [first payoff]       Closes: planted / Opens: mid loop
4.  "Second Idea"      ~70s   Delivers: [second payoff]      Escalates stakes
5.  "The Turn"         ~80s   Delivers: [unexpected angle]   Closes: mid / Opens: pre-CTA loop
6.  "Payoff + CTA"     ~45s   Answers: [the hook]            Closes: main + pre-CTA loop
```

Write this scene list, confirm it covers the right payoffs and the loop chain holds,
then proceed to Step 4 — narration.

## Animation patterns that engage the viewer

**Rule:** One scene = one pattern. Never mix two patterns in one scene.
If the content requires two patterns, that is two scenes. Varying the pattern
scene-to-scene IS the visual engagement beat (the 30–90s rhythm above).

For each pattern: when to use it, why it engages, what the animation looks like, and
when NOT to use it. Examples are illustrative — fill them with your real, sourced facts.

---

### Pattern 1 — Race Track + Scoreboard

**WHY IT ENGAGES:** Competition activates attention instinctively. Two things racing
triggers "who wins?" — the viewer leans forward and cannot look away until the result lands.

**WHEN TO USE:**
- Comparing two models, products, tools, or companies on one metric
- Showing a winner-takes-all result
- Any scene whose title contains "vs," "compared," or "which is better"

**ANIMATION:**
Two horizontal bars (D.cyan for one entity, D.violet for the other), starting from
zero width. Bars extend left-to-right simultaneously. The trailing bar catches and
overtakes mid-animation. Labels float at the bar end showing the metric value. Final
position lands with a snappy spring (damping 20, stiffness 200). Scoreboard tile
above the bars — label + final value for each.
`audio_anchor`: the spoken metric number.

**DO NOT USE:**
- When comparing more than 2 things (use 2×2 Matrix)
- When the comparison is close and undramatic (use Split Panel)
- When the point is a process, not a competition (use Build-Up Bullets)

---

### Pattern 2 — Single Large Number

**WHY IT ENGAGES:** Extreme magnitudes break intuition. A number filling ~40% of the
screen says "this is the only thing that matters right now." A counter ticking to the
final value creates anticipation.

**WHEN TO USE:**
- When you have exactly ONE number that is the entire point of the scene
- Very large, very small, or self-contradictory numbers
- Cost shocks, waste numbers, performance surprises
- The hook scene — a single number as the cold open

**ANIMATION:**
Canvas-wide layout. Number centered, 20–30% of canvas height, D.amber (cost/warning),
D.red (failure), or D.green (success). Counter ticks from 0 to final value over ~30
frames with easeOutCubic. Final value lands with a stamp — heavy spring (damping 12,
mass 2). Label below in D.text_dim, smaller.
`audio_anchor`: the spoken number.

**DO NOT USE:**
- For comparing two numbers (use Race Track)
- For showing a trend over time (use Timeline)
- For round, unimpressive numbers

---

### Pattern 3 — Build-Up Bullets (Numbered Steps)

**WHY IT ENGAGES:** Progress and completion instincts. Each step appearing signals
forward motion; the viewer stays to see all steps revealed, like a countdown.

**WHEN TO USE:**
- How something works mechanically (3–6 steps)
- A decision framework (the rules)
- A list of features, capabilities, or failure modes
- Any scene whose narration sounds like "first… then… finally…"

**ANIMATION:**
Items enter one at a time, staggered 12–15 frames each. Each item: icon/number left
(D.cyan), label right (D.text_dim). Entrance: snappy spring from left (damping 20,
stiffness 200). As each new item enters, previous items dim slightly (opacity 0.7).
Final state: all at full opacity.
`audio_anchor`: the phrase introducing each step.

**DO NOT USE:**
- For 2-item comparisons (use Race Track)
- For more than 6 items (split the scene)
- When items have no clear order

---

### Pattern 4 — Named Metaphor Object (Tank / Pipe / Valve)

**WHY IT ENGAGES:** Physical intuition transfers to abstract concepts instantly.
Viewers understand water flowing between tanks without explanation — the metaphor does
the work narration alone cannot. It makes invisible processes visible.

**WHEN TO USE:**
- Any physical process: flow, transfer, fill, drain, overflow, blockage
- Abstract concepts with a real-world physical analogue (data migration, memory, budget)
- The first BODY scene — establishes the visual language for the whole video

**ANIMATION:**
Name the object specifically — not "a container," a "glass cylinder with measurement
marks." Fill level animates from source (D.violet) to destination (D.cyan) over 40–60
frames. Pipe between containers: particles flow along the path, D.amber. Valve handle
rotates open at the `audio_anchor` moment. Labels state what each container represents.
`audio_anchor`: the moment the transfer begins.

**DO NOT USE:**
- When there is no real physical analogue (don't force a metaphor)
- For step-by-step processes (use Build-Up Bullets)
- For comparisons (use Race Track)

---

### Pattern 5 — Quote Card

**WHY IT ENGAGES:** Social proof and identification. A named real human said something
about this topic. Viewers trust another human's experience over abstract claims.

**WHEN TO USE:**
- You have a verbatim quote from a named person (from the research step)
- The quote is more credible than any statistic
- Testimonial scenes — showing the problem is widely felt, not theoretical

**ANIMATION:**
Photo/avatar left (40% width), quote card right (60% width). Photo spring-enters from
left, snappy (damping 20). Quote text typewriter-writes in D.text_dim, one word every
~3 frames. Name and role below — D.cyan name, D.text_dim role. Large quotation marks
in background, D.violet at 15% opacity.
`audio_anchor`: the first distinctive word of the quote.

**DO NOT USE:**
- When you don't have a real, verifiable quote (DO NOT fabricate)
- For statistics (use Single Large Number)
- As the hook scene — quotes build credibility mid-video, not at the start

---

### Pattern 6 — Split Panel Before/After

**WHY IT ENGAGES:** Direct comparison eliminates interpretation. The viewer sees both
states at once and draws their own conclusion. The contrast does the persuasion.

**WHEN TO USE:**
- Before/after a change (refactor, upgrade, process improvement)
- Two states of the same thing at different points in time
- When you have real numbers for BOTH sides (required)

**ANIMATION:**
Canvas splits vertically at center. Left: D.violet background, "BEFORE" label. Right:
D.cyan background, "AFTER" label. The key metric appears in each panel — large,
centered. A slider line at center wipes left-to-right at the `audio_anchor` moment,
revealing the "after" state. Slider has a D.amber handle.
`audio_anchor`: the moment the transformation is revealed.

**DO NOT USE:**
- When you don't have real numbers for both sides
- For 3+ states (use Timeline)
- When the comparison is a competition (use Race Track — it shows a winner; Split Panel shows a transformation)

---

### Pattern 7 — Timeline Spine

**WHY IT ENGAGES:** Chronology creates cause-and-effect understanding. Events on a
spine reveal that today's state was built step by step. Staggered node reveals create
rhythm; the viewer anticipates the next node.

**WHEN TO USE:**
- History of a technology, company, or product family
- "How we got here" context scenes
- Showing a trend has been building for years
- Comparing release dates of competing products

**ANIMATION:**
Horizontal spine across 70% of canvas width, D.text_dim. Nodes pop in left-to-right,
staggered ~18 frames each, bouncy spring (damping 8). Each node: circle (D.cyan for
positive events, D.red for setbacks), label above (event), date below (D.text_dim).
`audio_anchor`: the date or event name as spoken.

**DO NOT USE:**
- For more than 7 nodes (use two scenes)
- When time order doesn't matter (use Build-Up Bullets)
- For future projections (timeline is for verified historical facts only)

---

### Pattern 8 — 2×2 Matrix (Decision Framework)

**WHY IT ENGAGES:** Decision pressure. The matrix tells the viewer where THEY sit.
Viewers immediately scan for themselves — "which quadrant am I in?" That personal
identification keeps them engaged.

**WHEN TO USE:**
- A decision framework: "which option for which situation?"
- Categorising entities into four types on two dimensions
- The verdict scene — the framework for the viewer's specific situation

**ANIMATION:**
Four equal quadrants. Grid lines draw first (D.text_dim, thin). Axis labels at extremes
(D.text_dim). Each quadrant fills with a faint D.color tint matching its meaning.
Content cards enter quadrant by quadrant (top-left → top-right → bottom-left →
bottom-right), staggered ~20 frames each, snappy spring. The viewer's recommended
quadrant pulses with a D.amber border glow at the end.
`audio_anchor`: the axis label when spoken.

**DO NOT USE:**
- When you have fewer than 4 meaningful categories (use Build-Up Bullets)
- When the axes aren't clear and named
- When there's a clear winner across all quadrants (use Race Track)

## Guidelines

### Always
- Write the scene list and confirm it BEFORE writing any narration
- Build the list payoff-first: write each scene's "Delivers/Answers" before its title
- Map the open-loop chain — note which loop each scene opens and closes
- Plan a transition INTO each scene — a bridge that carries the thread across the cut
- Make every scene re-hook: it must raise a question OR answer a prior one
- Place a strong re-hook near the ~2-minute mark and an engagement beat every 30–90s
- Front-load the strongest fact/visual; escalate stakes toward the turn
- Assign one animation pattern per scene from the 8 above
- Name the `audio_anchor` phrase when designing each scene
- Answer the Scene 1 hook in the final scene — explicitly

### Quality bar — verify before proceeding to Step 4
- [ ] Scene count is within the range for the target video length
- [ ] Scene 1 is a cold open / hook that lands within the first 15 seconds
- [ ] Scene N answers the Scene 1 hook directly
- [ ] At no point are all loops closed before the final scene
- [ ] Every scene opens or closes a loop (none is a dead "valley")
- [ ] Every scene boundary has a planned transition (passes the boundary test)
- [ ] One idea per scene (no scene title requires "and")
- [ ] Every body scene has a named animation pattern from the 8 above
- [ ] No two adjacent scenes use the same pattern
- [ ] There is a re-hook near the ~2-minute mark

If any item fails → revise the scene list. Do not proceed to narration with a weak structure.
