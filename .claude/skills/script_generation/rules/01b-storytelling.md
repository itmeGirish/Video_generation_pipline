# Step 3b — Storytelling

Facts inform. Stories are remembered. The same information delivered as a story is far
more memorable and far more watchable than a list of points. After the scene structure
exists (rule 01), layer a narrative over it so the video feels like a story being told,
not a topic being covered.

This is a LAYER, not a replacement: the scene arc and loop chain from rule 01 still hold.
Storytelling gives those scenes a spine, stakes, and an emotional arc.

## Why story beats facts

A story is information wrapped in cause, stakes, and change. It works because:
- **Cause-and-effect is sticky** — the brain holds "X happened, so Y" far better than "X. Y. Z."
- **Stakes create investment** — the viewer keeps watching to see how it turns out.
- **Emotion makes it memorable** — a felt moment is recalled long after a stated fact.
- **Transformation gives a payoff** — a before→after the viewer can feel landing.

A pile of true facts with no story is a lecture. Viewers leave lectures.

---

## The cause-and-effect chain — the core test

The single most important storytelling move: scenes connect by **"because of that,"** not
**"and then."** Each scene should be a consequence of the previous one, not just the next
item on a list.

- ❌ "And then" structure: fact A, and then fact B, and then fact C (a list — forgettable)
- ✅ "Because of that" structure: A happened → *because of that* B → *because of that* C
  (a chain — the viewer is pulled forward by consequence)

**Test:** read the scene list and put "because of that" between each scene. If it still
makes sense, you have a story. If you can only say "and then," you have a list — re-order
or re-frame so each scene is a consequence of the last. (This is the same thread the
continuity check in rule 05b looks for.)

## Framework 1 — Hook, Build, Payoff (three-act)

The YouTube-native three-act shape:
- **Hook (Act 1 — setup):** establish the situation and the problem/question fast. The
  normal world + the disruption that starts the story.
- **Build (Act 2 — confrontation):** raise the stakes, explore the conflict, complicate
  it. This is the body — tension rising, not flat exposition.
- **Payoff (Act 3 — resolution):** deliver the answer and the transformation. The hook's
  question resolved, the change made tangible.

Maps onto rule 01's arc: Hook = Scene 1; Build = body + the turn; Payoff = verdict.

## Framework 2 — The story spine (cause-and-effect beats)

Pixar's story spine is the cleanest cause-and-effect template. Adapt it to the topic:

```
"Once upon a time…"   — the normal state / the status quo
"Every day…"          — how things normally work (the assumption)
"Until one day…"      — the disruption: the surprising fact, the change, the problem
"Because of that…"    — the first consequence
"Because of that…"    — the escalating consequence
"Until finally…"      — the climax: the answer / the turn
"Ever since then…"    — the new normal / what it means for the viewer now
```

For an explainer, the "until one day" is usually your hook fact, and the "because of
that" beats are your body scenes. The spine guarantees a cause-and-effect chain instead
of a list. (Fill each beat with your real topic and researched facts — don't keep the
fairy-tale wording on screen.)

## The viewer is the hero, you are the guide

In an explainer, **the viewer is the hero** — they have the problem, the question, the
goal. You (the narrator) are the GUIDE who helps them get there, not the hero of your own
story.

- Frame the problem as THE VIEWER'S problem ("you've hit this wall…"), not an abstract one.
- The payoff is the viewer's transformation: what they can now do/understand/decide.
- Don't make the video about how clever the explanation is — make it about the viewer's win.

This pairs with the direct-address "you" voice in rule 02 and the value test in rule 06.

## Stakes, conflict, transformation

Three ingredients that turn information into story — every video needs all three:

- **Stakes** — why it matters. What the viewer gains by knowing this, or loses by not.
  No stakes = no reason to care.
- **Conflict / tension** — the problem, the contradiction, the obstacle, the "two things
  that can't both be true." Conflict is the engine; without it there's no story.
- **Transformation** — a before→after. The viewer (or the subject) ends somewhere
  different from where they started. Show the change, don't just assert it.

If a script has facts but none of these three, it's not yet a story — add the missing one.

## Applying story to a non-fiction explainer

You're not inventing fiction — you're finding the story that's already in the facts:

1. **Find the conflict in the topic** — the tension, the surprising contradiction, the
   problem someone faced (from research, rule 00).
2. **Pick the spine** — hook/build/payoff for most; the story spine when there's a clear
   sequence of consequences.
3. **Set the stakes in Scene 1** — make the viewer feel why this matters before the body.
4. **Chain the scenes with "because of that"** — each scene a consequence of the last.
5. **Land the transformation in the payoff** — the before→after, the viewer's win.
6. **Use a concrete subject when you can** — a named person, a real case, a single
   example to follow is more gripping than abstract explanation (and pairs with the
   "show, don't tell" and example rules).

## Guidelines

### Always
- Layer a narrative over the scene structure — don't deliver facts as a list
- Connect scenes with "because of that," not "and then"
- Give the script stakes, conflict, and a transformation
- Make the viewer the hero; be the guide
- Find the story already in the researched facts — don't invent unsourced drama

### Never
- Ship a flat list of facts with no cause-and-effect chain
- Make the video about the cleverness of the explanation instead of the viewer's win
- Add fictional or unsourced "story" details just for drama (accuracy still rules — 00 / 06b)
- Force a story spine that doesn't fit — use hook/build/payoff if the spine feels artificial
