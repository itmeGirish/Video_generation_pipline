# YouTube Strategy — Pre-Production

**This runs before rule 01 (scene structure). Before any narration. Before any bullets.**

Average creators pick a topic and start writing. Top creators spend 40-60% of their time
here — on title, thesis, thumbnail, and architecture — before a single word of script is written.

The script serves the strategy. Not the other way around.

---

## Step 1 — Write the title BEFORE the script

The title is not a label for the finished video. It is a **creative brief** for the script.
Write it first. Everything in the script must earn its place by serving the title's promise.

### Title formula

> **DERIVE DON'T COPY — examples below show the PATTERN, not the content.**
> Never write a title that uses the same topic, number, or entity as an example here.
> Apply the pattern to YOUR topic's research findings.

The highest-performing titles follow one of these patterns:

| Pattern | AI/tech example | Non-tech example | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contradiction** | `"Why the Smartest AI Lies the Most"` | `"Why the Safest Drug Has the Worst Side Effects"` | Seems wrong → must watch to resolve |
| **Impossible number** | `"GPT-5.5 Fabricates 86% of the Time"` | `"97% of Diets Fail Within 5 Years. Here's Why."` | Specific number + explanation promise |
| **The vs with a twist** | `"GPT vs Claude — They're Not Even Competing"` | `"Harvard vs State School — Wrong Question"` | Reframes the obvious comparison |
| **Thing nobody explains** | `"The Real Reason Kubernetes is Hard"` | `"The Real Reason Antibiotics Stop Working"` | Promises unique angle |
| **Cost nobody talks about** | `"The $0.06 Call That Cost a Lawyer $500K"` | `"The Free Drug That Costs $80,000 a Year"` | Stakes + specificity |
| **Result that surprises** | `"I Migrated 12M Rows. Here's What Broke."` | `"I Ran Every Diet for 6 Months. Here's What Worked."` | Personal + specific + unexpected |

**Apply the pattern to your topic's hook fact from research (rule 00).**
The title comes from what you found in research — not from choosing a pattern and filling in generic words.

### Title quality checklist

- [ ] Contains a number OR a contradiction OR the word "why/how/actually/real"
- [ ] Specific enough that a viewer knows EXACTLY what the video covers
- [ ] Creates a question the viewer wants answered
- [ ] Does NOT contain "everything you need to know", "ultimate guide", "beginners guide" — these perform poorly for explainer content
- [ ] Can be understood in 3 seconds — no jargon that requires domain knowledge to parse
- [ ] Under 60 characters (YouTube truncates at ~60 in search results)

### Test the title before writing the script

Ask: *"If I saw this title with a strong thumbnail, would I click it over the other results?"*
If uncertain — rewrite the title. A weak title means weak views regardless of content quality.

## Step 1b — Click confirmation (MrBeast rule)

The title makes a promise. Scene 1 must CONFIRM that promise within the first 3 seconds.

If the title says *"Why the Smartest AI Lies the Most"* — second 3 must show the contradiction, not explain what the video is about.
If the title says *"I Migrated 12M Rows"* — second 3 must show the migration, not introduce yourself.

**The viewer's first question is: "Did I click the right video?"**
Answer YES immediately. Viewers who suspect clickbait leave in under 5 seconds.

| Title promise | Scene 1 opener (correct) | Scene 1 opener (wrong) |
|---|---|---|
| "Why the smartest AI lies most" | State the contradiction cold: both facts, no context | "Today I want to talk about AI hallucination..." |
| "GPT-5.5 fabricates 86% of the time" | Open with "eighty-six percent" — no introduction | "AI models have gotten very powerful recently..." |
| "Kubernetes is hard for the wrong reason" | State the wrong reason immediately | "Kubernetes is a container orchestration platform..." |

**Check:** Cover the rest of the script. Read only Scene 1 sentence 1.
Does it confirm the title's promise? If not — rewrite sentence 1.

## Step 2 — Identify the thumbnail moment BEFORE writing

The thumbnail is the #1 factor in click-through rate. It is not chosen after editing.
It is designed before the script is written, then the script creates that moment.

### What makes a high-CTR thumbnail for tech explainers

**Option A — The conflict visual:**
Two entities side by side with visual contrast. One wins, one loses.
Example: CYAN vs MAGENTA bars, one dramatically taller. Number visible.

**Option B — The impossible number:**
A single large number that seems wrong. Red. Centered. No context — context is in the title.
Example: `86%` in massive red text on black background.

**Option C — The before/after:**
Left side: chaos/bad state. Right side: clean/good state.
Divider line between them. Immediate visual story.

**Option D — The face + reaction (if creator appears on camera):**
Strong emotion aligned with title. Surprise, disbelief, or "I figured it out" expression.
Not relevant for animation-only pipeline — use Option A, B, or C.

### Thumbnail decision

Before writing the script, decide:
1. Which thumbnail option will you use?
2. Which specific number or visual contrast is the thumbnail?
3. Which scene in the script creates that frame?

Then make sure the script ACTUALLY creates that frame — with the right visual, the right number,
the right color contrast. The thumbnail drives the script requirement.

Write it here:
```
Thumbnail moment: Scene [N], Bullet [M]
Visual: [describe the thumbnail frame in one sentence]
Number/element shown: [the specific thing visible in thumbnail]
```

## Step 3 — Write the thesis (not the topic)

**Topic:** What the video is about.
**Thesis:** What the video ARGUES.

Every top creator has a thesis. Average creators have topics.

| Topic (weak) | Thesis (strong) |
|---|---|
| "GPT-5.5 vs Claude comparison" | "GPT-5.5 and Claude are not competing — they're built for completely different failure modes" |
| "How transformers work" | "The transformer's real innovation wasn't attention — it was making everything run in parallel" |
| "Kubernetes is complicated" | "Kubernetes is hard because it solves the wrong problem for 80% of companies using it" |
| "Database migration tips" | "Every failed migration fails for one of three reasons — knowing which one saves you 48 hours" |

### Thesis requirements

A strong thesis:
- Takes a side (not "both have pros and cons")
- Would make some viewers disagree (mild controversy = more comments)
- Can be stated in one sentence
- Is NOT obvious from a Google search of the topic
- Is something the creator actually believes from research, not a clickbait take

Write the thesis here before writing any scene:
```
THESIS: [one sentence — what this video argues]
```

Every scene must serve this thesis. If a scene doesn't advance the argument — cut it.

## Step 4 — Design the open loops

**Open loop:** A question raised and intentionally not answered yet.
Viewers who have an unanswered question in their head cannot stop watching.

Top creators plant 3-5 open loops throughout a video.
The final scene resolves the most important one.

### Open loop types

**Type 1 — The hook loop (Scene 1):**
The central contradiction or question the whole video answers.
Example: *"I'll show you exactly why in about 4 minutes."*
→ Viewer must watch 4 more minutes to get the answer.

**Type 2 — The planted loop (early body scene):**
A secondary question raised mid-video, answered 2-3 scenes later.
Example: *"There's a number that explains this completely. I'll get to it in a moment."*
→ Viewer keeps watching for the number.

**Type 3 — The mid-video loop (at the 40% mark):**
The most surprising fact dropped at 40% through, with a consequence held for later.
Example: *"That's what the benchmark says. But here's what it means in practice — and that's where it gets genuinely strange."*
→ Viewer must reach 60% to get the "genuinely strange" part.

**Type 4 — The pre-CTA loop:**
A question in the second-to-last scene that the last scene resolves.
Example: *"So which one do you actually pick? I'll give you a framework in a second."*
→ Viewer watches the CTA scene because they need the framework.

### Open loop map

Before writing, map all 4 loops:

```
Loop 1 (Hook):        Scene 1     — resolved in Scene [N]
Loop 2 (Planted):     Scene [X]   — resolved in Scene [X+2]
Loop 3 (Mid-video):   Scene [40%] — resolved in Scene [60%]
Loop 4 (Pre-CTA):     Scene [N-1] — resolved in Scene [N]
```

Then CHECK that the scenes actually plant and resolve each loop.

## Step 5 — Identify the one shareable insight

Every video that spreads has ONE insight that viewers want to tell someone else.
One framework. One fact. One phrase. Not three. One.

It is usually:
- A framework so simple it fits on a sticky note: *"Choose the failure mode you can survive"*
- A fact so surprising it's almost unbelievable: *"86% of the time, when it's wrong, it invents an answer"*
- A reframe that makes something obvious in hindsight: *"They're not competing — they're built for different failure modes"*

Write it here:
```
SHAREABLE INSIGHT: [the one thing viewers will repeat to a colleague tomorrow]
```

Then check: does the script deliver this insight with enough emphasis (pause, visual hold, animation)?
It should appear in the narration with a `<pause 0.5s+>` after it and a REPLACE visual that makes it
impossible to miss.

## Step 6 — Pre-script strategy summary

Before proceeding to rule 01 (scene structure), have these 5 things written:

```
TITLE:              [final title under 60 chars]
THESIS:             [one sentence — what the video argues]
THUMBNAIL:          Scene N, Bullet M — [describe the frame]
SHAREABLE INSIGHT:  [the one thing viewers will repeat]
OPEN LOOPS:
  Hook:     Scene 1  → resolved Scene [N]
  Planted:  Scene [X] → resolved Scene [X+2]
  Mid:      Scene [~40%] → resolved Scene [~60%]
  Pre-CTA:  Scene [N-1] → resolved Scene [N]
```

If any of these 5 are missing or weak — do not start writing scenes.
A weak strategy produces a weak video regardless of how well the script is written.

## What top creators know that average creators don't

**MKBHD:** Lead with the most impressive thing. Don't make the viewer wait for the reveal.
**Fireship:** Respect the audience's intelligence. Skip the basics they already know.
**3Blue1Brown:** The visual IS the explanation — if the animation doesn't teach, cut it.
**CGP Grey:** Spend 10x more time on research than on writing. The unique angle comes from depth.
**Tom Scott:** Specific > General. "A vending machine in Tokyo" beats "some vending machines".
**Kurzgesagt:** One metaphor, carried through the whole video. Don't mix metaphors.

Apply all six to this script:
- Lead with the most impressive thing (MKBHD)
- Skip what the audience already knows (Fireship)
- Every animation teaches, not just decorates (3Blue1Brown)
- The unique angle comes from research depth (CGP Grey)
- Use specific names, numbers, events — not "some", "many", "often" (Tom Scott)
- One central metaphor, consistent throughout (Kurzgesagt)
